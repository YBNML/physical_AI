### VLA :: Published hierarchical / dual-system VLA architectures: interfaces, rates, and what the ablation evidence actually shows about the high/low split — evaluated against a proposed "cerebrum (RGB+EE pose → target EE pose) / cerebellum (target pose + joints + RGB → joint commands)" two-model local design

-- KEY TAKEAWAYS --
 * No published system demonstrates that a high/low SPLIT beats a monolithic model of comparable total size at a MATCHED control rate. I searched specifically for this and it does not exist. Every reported win decomposes into one of three other causes: (a) latency/rate decoupling, (b) the split creating a place to inject cheap non-teleop data, or (c) pretraining and co-training recipes.
 * The single best-controlled test is HiRT's Table 1: on real-world quasi-static tasks, hierarchical HiRT scored 70.0 average vs monolithic Vanilla-VLA 71.3 — the split was SLIGHTLY WORSE. The split won only on DYNAMIC tasks (75 vs 48), where the monolith's 4.1 Hz was the binding constraint.
 * π0.5's own ablation is the clearest self-refutation of the runtime hierarchy: the second-best configuration was 'implicit HL' — NO high-level inference at runtime, but subtask-prediction data still in the training mixture. Their conclusion verbatim: 'a significant portion of that benefit is already obtained simply by including subtask prediction data in the training mixture.'
 * OpenHelix's asynchrony sweep found that running the high level every step vs ONCE PER 60-STEP EPISODE barely changes success rate (e.g. task1: 94/97/95/95/95/95/95). Their probing showed why: the latent passed down mostly encodes the INSTRUCTION and is 'largely insensitive to changes in visual information'. In practice, existing System 2s are not doing closed-loop spatial reasoning.
 * The interfaces actually used, exhaustively: single continuous LATENT VECTOR (Helix, HiRT via MAP-pooled last layer, GR00T N1 via 12th-layer hidden states + cross-attention, RoboDual, LCB's <ACT> token); NATURAL LANGUAGE (RT-H's 'move arm forward', π0.5's subtask string, Gemini Robotics 1.5's orchestrator→VLA tool call); 2D PATH/SKETCH (RT-Trajectory, HAMSTER); SE(3) END-EFFECTOR POSES (RT-Affordance — the only one matching your proposal, and it renders them visually onto the image rather than passing raw numbers); DISCRETIZED ACTION TOKENS (RoboDual, alongside latents); SHARED PARAMETERS / no serialized interface at all (π0, FiS-VLA).
 * RT-Affordance is the closest published analogue to your Model A and it works dramatically: 68% (predicted poses) / 76% (oracle poses) vs 28% for flat RT-2 and 24% for goal-image conditioning with a LARGER backbone; 70% vs 3% on non-grasping tasks. But its own Table III shows the affordance predictor drops from 77% to 24% without ~750 cheap annotated images and to 11% without web co-training. The split's value was DATA ACCESS, not parameter allocation.
 * Rate can be bought without any split. SmolVLA's async inference stack — same single model, just decoupling chunk prediction from chunk execution — gave equal success rates but ~30% faster completion (9.7s vs 13.75s) and 19 vs 9 cycles in fixed time. OpenVLA-OFT made a 7B MONOLITH 26x faster at action generation with 3x lower latency, then beat π0, Octo, and Diffusion Policy at 97.1% on LIBERO.
 * The two-model interface is the dominant failure mode, not a detail. OpenHelix Table 6: WITHOUT pre-aligning the projector between the MLLM and the policy, every configuration scored 0/0/0/0/0. With pre-alignment, 94–96% first-task success. Naive joint training of two separately-pretrained models collapses.
 * Where hierarchical splits are real, they are extremely LOPSIDED and the low level is TINY: Helix 7B / 80M; RoboDual 7B / ~20M trainable; π0 and π0.5 2–3B VLM / 300M action expert; GR00T N1 1.34B VLM / ~0.86B. Nobody splits capacity 50/50. And when NVIDIA wanted more performance in N1.6 they DOUBLED the low-level DiT (16→32 layers) and FROZE more of the high level.
 * Almost no learned low-level policy in this literature outputs joint values. HiRT outputs EE relative position + gripper; RT-1/RT-2/RT-A output Cartesian EE deltas; TRI's LBM outputs relative SE(3) EE poses with 6D rotation; GR00T N1.6 explicitly moved to 'state-relative action chunks... rather than absolute joint angles'. π0.5 does command joint targets, but tracked by trivial PD controllers at 50 Hz with no planning.
 * The field's other consistent finding is that the low level should have ITS OWN fresh perception, not just the high level's output. OpenHelix makes this its definition of a dual system and uses it to EXCLUDE π0 and GR00T N1 from the category. Your Model B does take RGB, which is correct.
 * Evaluation rigor is the binding constraint on answering this question yourself. TRI used 50 real rollouts per task per policy per condition and 1,800 total trials with Bayesian analysis and Bonferroni correction to separate policies. Google ran >90% of Gemini Robotics 1.5 development evals in simulation.

-- IMPLICATIONS --
 > Your Model A (RGB + current EE pose → target EE pose) is well-supported: it is essentially RT-Affordance's affordance generator, and the evidence for a pose-shaped intermediate is the strongest in this whole literature (68% vs 28% at matched backbone and data). Keep it. But copy RT-Affordance's actual source of leverage: train it on cheap non-teleop data (web images, hand-annotated snapshots — they used ~750 images collected in 1h and labeled in 2h). If Model A only ever sees your own teleop episodes, you pay the split's costs and get none of its benefit.
 > Your Model B (target pose + joints + RGB → JOINT commands) is the weak half and is not supported by anything I found. No published hierarchical VLA asks a learned low level to convert a Cartesian target into joint commands — that is inverse kinematics, which is analytically solved for a known arm. RT-Affordance's low level is Cartesian; HiRT's outputs EE deltas; TRI's LBM outputs relative SE(3); GR00T N1.6 explicitly abandoned absolute joint angles. Strongly consider: Model B outputs an EE delta/impedance reference, and an analytic IK + PD/impedance controller does the joint mapping — optionally with a small learned residual. (Supporting, though RL not VLA: Varin, Grossman & Kuindersma, 'A Comparison of Action Spaces for Learning Manipulation Tasks', found learning references for a TASK-SPACE IMPEDANCE controller significantly reduces sample complexity vs joint torque or joint PD across peg insertion, hammering, and pushing, under both PPO and SAC.)
 > Your stated goal — 'split capacity across two models to IMPROVE task success/generalization' — is the one claim the literature does not support. π0.5 tested it and found the training data explains most of it; HiRT tested it and the monolith was marginally better on static tasks; GR00T N1 and Helix publish no such comparison at all. Reframe the goal to what IS supported: split for LATENCY, for CHEAP HIGH-LEVEL DATA, and for HUMAN-INSPECTABLE INTERVENTION.
 > The local-hardware plan has a specific problem. Helix gets its 7–9 Hz / 200 Hz decoupling by running S1 and S2 on DEDICATED GPUs; Gemini Robotics puts the backbone in the CLOUD. On one Apple-silicon Mac mini, two models share one unified memory pool and one compute unit. If they run sequentially, total latency = latency(A) + latency(B), which is strictly worse than one monolith of the same total size. You only win if Model A runs on a genuinely separate schedule (async, cached output) — which OpenHelix says is fine, since high-level update frequency barely mattered.
 > Before building two models, get the rate the cheap way and measure. Implement SmolVLA-style async inference on ONE model: action chunking + a queue drained to ~70% before triggering the next inference + near-duplicate observation filtering. That gave equal success and ~30% faster completion / 2x throughput with no second model. If that closes your rate gap, the split is unnecessary. OpenVLA-OFT's 26x speedup on a 7B monolith (parallel decoding, chunking, continuous actions, L1 regression) is the other lever.
 > If you do build the split, do NOT train the two models independently and bolt them together. OpenHelix got 0% success across every configuration without projector pre-alignment. The three viable recipes are: (1) Helix — train fully end-to-end, backpropagating from S1 into S2 through the latent; (2) OpenHelix — two-stage, freeze the high level, pre-align the projector, then prompt-tune; (3) GR00T N1.5 — freeze the VLM entirely and train only the adapter and the action model. Recipe (3) is the most tractable on a Mac mini and produced N1's largest gains (real GR-1 success 43.3% → 83.0%).
 > An explicit SE(3) pose interface has one large advantage the latent-vector systems lack, and it is the thing that actually paid off in RT-H: correction and debugging. RT-H's biggest single number was not the hierarchy (+15%) but language-motion intervention learning (40% → 63%, beating IWR by 50%). A pose interface is inspectable in RViz, correctable with a 6-DoF mouse, and unit-testable. Budget for that as the deliverable, not for a success-rate win.
 > Consider the cheaper architecture that dominates in 2025: one model, two weight-sets, one forward pass (π0's action expert; FiS-VLA sharing the last 2 transformer blocks). You get action-path specialization without a serialized interface, without a second forward pass, and without the joint-training collapse risk. FiS-VLA hits 117.7 Hz this way with chunk size 8.
 > Supervise the interface explicitly, and consider an auxiliary head. OpenHelix's Table 7 showed that forcing the intermediate latent to predict position/rotation via an auxiliary task lifted CALVIN average length from 3.45 to 4.01 — the single largest gain in their study. Your design gets this for free because the interface IS a pose. That is a genuine, evidence-backed advantage of your pose interface over a free-form latent.
 > Don't run Model A fast. OpenHelix found essentially flat success from step=1 to step=60 (once per episode). RT-H found fixed-frequency high-level updates worse than one-step-ahead async prediction because skills change at precise moments — so prefer event-triggered replanning (on gripper state change, on large pose error, on subtask completion) over a fixed high rate. RT-Affordance similarly replans 'at fixed or adaptive intervals'.
 > Plan the evaluation before the architecture. With TRI needing 50 rollouts per task per condition, and HiRT's monolith-vs-split gap being 1.3 points on static tasks, a 10-trial comparison on one arm cannot distinguish your two designs. Build the monolithic baseline FIRST at matched total parameters and matched effective control rate; test on DYNAMIC scenes (moving targets, disturbances) where every paper's hierarchy advantage actually shows up; and report enough trials to have power.

### INTERFACE :: The right interface between a high-level ("cerebrum") and low-level ("cerebellum") robot manipulation policy — evaluating a Cartesian end-effector pose as the hand-off representation

-- KEY TAKEAWAYS --
 * The exact representation proposed — an ABSOLUTE Cartesian EE pose — is the worst-scoring of the four canonical action abstractions in the only clean head-to-head I found: 69.0% (EE absolute) vs 77.3% (joint absolute) vs 88.0% (joint delta) vs 89.6% (EE delta) on a real single-arm platform with ACT-regression. Chunk-wise delta (relative to the pose at chunk start) beats step-wise delta by ~10 points, with an O(k) vs O(1) noise-amplification argument behind it. Changing 'absolute pose' to 'chunk-wise delta pose' is the single highest-value edit to the proposal.
 * Cartesian EE pose as a hierarchical interface IS well-precedented and works: HDP (80.18% vs 15-18% for flat DP/ACT on RLBench), GHOST (80% vs 10% for flat Diffusion Policy on fold-onesie), RT-Affordance (69% vs 15% for RT-2). The proposal is not a novel or unsupported idea — it is roughly HDP's architecture.
 * But in every successful system the pose is NOT passed as a bare 7-vector. GHOST projects the 3D goal into each camera's image plane as a dense end-effector heatmap and found this beat single-pixel masks; RT-Affordance overlays the pose on the image; RT-Trajectory draws a 2.5D curve. If Model B consumes RGB anyway, render Model A's target into that RGB.
 * Every large-scale production dual-system VLA — GR00T N1, Helix, Fast-in-Slow — uses a LATENT token interface, jointly trained end-to-end, not an explicit pose. GR00T N1 passes layer-12 vision-language embeddings, System 2 at 10 Hz, System 1 at 120 Hz. None of them published an ablation against an explicit pose interface, so this is convention rather than proven superiority — but it is a strong prior.
 * RoboDual is the best compromise and the architecture I would recommend copying: it passes BOTH latent features AND the generalist's coarse discretized EE-delta action. Results: +26.7% real-world over OpenVLA, +12% on CALVIN, strong performance with only 5% of demonstration data, and 3.8× higher control frequency (15 Hz).
 * A pose alone is NOT a sufficient interface. It must minimally carry: (1) gripper state — HDP's interface is (a_trans, a_rot, a_grip) and the gripper bit is non-optional; (2) timing/duration or velocity — a waypoint with no time is not a trajectory and the low level cannot infer approach speed, which matters for contact; (3) for any contact task, stiffness/damping or a compliance-mode flag; (4) on a 7-DoF arm, a redundancy/null-space term.
 * The contact-rich evidence is unambiguous. VICES: plain EE pose control wins on free-space path following but variable EE-space impedance wins clearly on surface wiping; task-space impedance references 'significantly reduce the number of samples needed' across peg insertion, hammering and pushing. For sub-mm clearance insertion, 'relying solely on velocity or position controllers is insufficient, as the tolerance and the robot's precision are at the same level'. Force-aware VLAs quantify the gap: ForceVLA +23.2% over π0, FAVLA +38.0 points over vision-only π0.
 * Camera-frame vs base_link: camera frame measurably wins when extrinsics are known and static. OC-VLA: +8.0 (sim continuous), +13.8 (sim discrete), +10.0 (real fixed camera), and roughly half the degradation under a novel viewpoint (14% vs 29% drop). But the paper never studies calibration-error sensitivity, and typical hand-eye calibration lands at ~2.1 mm RMS / ~3.2° — a systematic bias baked into every absolute pose command. With a MOVING head, the camera frame becomes time-varying and the target silently slides; the active-perception literature's response is to fall back to a world/base frame for the action and predict head pose separately.
 * On a redundant arm, EE pose is an underdetermined command. Real-world imitation on 5 confined-space tasks: joint space 96%, EE-pose + explicit redundancy (ERJ) 94%, pure task space 8%, with 0% on 'take cup out of cabinet'. On standard unconstrained tasks ERJ matched task-space efficiency, so adding the redundancy term costs nothing.
 * The pose→joints problem is already solved without learning. Analytic/damped-least-squares IK plus operational-space or Cartesian-impedance control runs at 500 Hz–1 kHz, deterministically, with zero data. HDP's own ablation shows the learned low level's win came specifically from being kinematics-aware (joint output + differentiable-FK distillation): the pose-output diffuser had a 24.55% IK error rate. A learned Model B is only worth its parameters if it also does collision-aware redundancy resolution, contact adaptation, or visual servoing to cancel Model A's calibration bias.
 * GHOST's oracle ablation is the sharpest warning about where the split's effort should go: with an oracle high-level, success was 90%; deployed, 36.7%. The authors conclude the bottleneck is the high level's goal prediction, not the low-level controller. Adding capacity to Model B optimizes the part that is already near-saturated.
 * The real, well-evidenced benefit of the split is ASYNCHRONY, not accuracy. OpenHelix swept MLLM inference from every 1 to every 60 environment steps and found performance 'quite similar' — the high level can run 60× slower for free. GR00T N1 runs 10 Hz / 120 Hz. RoboDual gets 3.8× control frequency. This is directly relevant to a Mac mini: it is the mechanism by which a big local model becomes deployable.
 * Do NOT train the two models separately and independently. OpenHelix: policy pretrained-then-finetuned scored 96/83/68 (avg length 3.53) vs from scratch 89/71/49 (2.85). GR00T N1 is 'tightly coupled and jointly trained end-to-end'. Also, the raw benefit of adding the high level over an already-strong low-level-only policy was small in OpenHelix's own numbers: 63.9% → 67.9% on 3-task chains.
 * Compute accounting matters and cuts against the proposal as specified. VLA-Perf's component breakdown for π0 shows the vision encoder + VLM backbone dominate (0.40 + 1.87 ms) over the action expert (0.91 ms). If Model B ALSO takes RGB and runs at high rate, you pay the expensive visual encoding twice and the split saves no compute. π0 achieves 19.0 Hz on Jetson Thor and 32.2 Hz on an RTX 4090; a Mac mini will be materially slower than either, so the asynchronous split is arguably necessary rather than optional — but only if Model B is small and does not re-encode images at 100+ Hz.

-- IMPLICATIONS --
 > KEEP the split, but change the interface from 'absolute SE(3) waypoint' to 'chunk-wise SE(3) DELTA + gripper + duration'. Delta beats absolute by ~20 points (89.6% vs 69.0%) and chunk-wise beats step-wise by ~10 points. Absolute pose also inherits the full ~2 mm/3° hand-eye calibration bias, which delta partly cancels.
 > Use a 6D continuous rotation representation (two 3-vectors + Gram-Schmidt), never Euler angles or raw quaternions. This is what Diffusion Policy uses for all positional-control action spaces, and it is the difference between a smooth and a discontinuous regression target near the wrap-around.
 > Deliver the target to Model B by RENDERING it into the RGB Model B already receives — as a Gaussian heatmap or overlay, following GHOST and RT-Affordance — rather than (or in addition to) concatenating a 7-vector to the state. GHOST found heatmaps beat binary masks; the mechanism is that the visual encoder can then attend to the goal spatially, in the same coordinate system as the observation.
 > Add a latent channel alongside the pose (the RoboDual pattern). A 6-D pose cannot express task phase, which object, 'be gentle', or 'this is the approach vs the insertion'. RoboDual's +26.7% real-world and 5%-data result is the best evidence that hybrid explicit+latent beats either alone. If you only pass a pose, Model B has no way to know why it is going there.
 > The interface MUST include, at minimum: target pose delta, gripper command (continuous, not binary, if the gripper supports it), and a time/duration or velocity scale. HDP's published interface is exactly (a_trans, a_rot, a_grip). A pose with no time is not executable — the low level must invent the approach speed, and approach speed is what determines impact force.
 > If ANY target task involves contact — insertion, wiping, door/drawer opening, cloth, wiping, screwing — add a stiffness/compliance channel (even a 3-level discrete flag: free-space / compliant-contact / stiff) and, if the hardware has an F/T sensor or joint torque sensing, feed force into Model B. The measured gains from doing so are large: +23.2% (ForceVLA over π0) to +38.0 points (FAVLA over vision-only π0). Without this, pose-only will look fine in demos and fail on anything precise.
 > Before building Model B, build the trivial baseline: Model A → damped-least-squares IK with joint-limit and singularity avoidance → joint position controller (or Cartesian impedance if the arm is torque-controlled). This runs at 1 kHz, needs zero data, and is what OSC has done since 1987. Then measure. HDP's 24.55% IK error rate is the honest estimate of how much headroom a learned Model B has, and that headroom exists mainly at singularities, joint limits, and unreachable targets — which a good IK solver with nullspace posture bias also mitigates.
 > Only build a learned Model B if it is doing at least one of: (a) collision-aware / redundancy-aware motion informed by the RGB scene (the ER paper's 8% vs 96% result shows this is real on 7-DoF arms), (b) contact/force adaptation, or (c) closed-loop visual servoing that cancels Model A's systematic calibration bias. If it is only doing IK, delete it and put all parameters into Model A.
 > On a 7-DoF arm, add an explicit redundancy term to the interface (elbow position or swivel angle — the ERJ formulation). It costs nothing on standard tasks (ERJ matched task-space sample efficiency on 8 RLBench tasks) and is the difference between 94% and 8% on confined-space tasks. On a 6-DoF arm this is much less critical.
 > Frame choice: use camera/head frame ONLY if the head is static during manipulation, or if you stamp every target with the head joint state at image-capture time and transform it in Model B. The measured benefit is real (+8 to +14 points, and halved degradation under novel viewpoints) but it is entirely contingent on knowing the extrinsics at the right instant. On a moving-head robot, the safe design is: Model A predicts in camera frame (for viewpoint invariance), you immediately convert to base_link using the synchronized head state, and Model B consumes base_link. You get the learning benefit without the moving-target hazard.
 > Budget the split for ASYNCHRONY, which is where the evidence is, not for accuracy. Target Model A at 2-10 Hz and Model B at 30-100+ Hz. OpenHelix's sweep says Model A can run as slow as every 60 low-level steps with essentially no loss. On a Mac mini this is the only realistic path to a usable control rate.
 > Make Model B SMALL and do not let it re-encode full-resolution RGB at high rate. VLA-Perf's breakdown shows vision encoding + VLM dominate latency; if both models do heavy vision you have doubled the expensive part and gained nothing. Options: give Model B a tiny wrist-camera encoder only, cache Model A's visual features, or run Model B on proprioception + rendered goal + a low-res crop.
 > Do not train the two models fully independently. At minimum pretrain Model B and then finetune it with Model A's actual (noisy) outputs as conditioning, so it sees the real input distribution. OpenHelix: pretrained+finetuned 3.53 avg length vs from-scratch 2.85. Training Model B on ground-truth poses and deploying it on Model A's predictions is a textbook covariate-shift setup and is exactly the gap GHOST measured (90% oracle vs 36.7% deployed).
 > Set expectations honestly on the 'improve performance' claim. Hierarchy gave huge gains where flat baselines were weak (HDP 80% vs DP 15%; GHOST 80% vs DP 10%) but only ~4 points where the flat low-level policy was already strong (OpenHelix 63.9% → 67.9%). And GHOST's oracle ablation says the high level is the bottleneck. The defensible claims for this project are: better control rate, better data efficiency, better interpretability/debuggability, and better generalization to new viewpoints — not automatically better task success.
 > Consider two cheaper alternatives before committing to two models: (1) action chunking alone (π0's H=50 at 50 Hz, Diffusion Policy's receding-horizon execution) already gives an implicit slow-think/fast-act split within one model; (2) Implicit Kinematic Policies supervises one policy in BOTH joint and Cartesian spaces simultaneously, capturing the same inductive bias without a second network. Measure against both.
 > Note the one axis where the Cartesian choice is clearly right and worth defending: cross-embodiment and transfer. The Demystifying paper found task-space 'exhibits a more pronounced advantage under cross-embodiment and transfer learning settings', and VICES found EE-space policies transferred from Franka Panda to Rethink Sawyer far better than joint-space ones. If the plan is ever to swap arms or reuse Model A across robots, the Cartesian interface is the reason to accept the ~20-point single-platform penalty — but you should accept it knowingly, and recover most of it by switching to deltas.

### CLASSICAL :: The classical (non-learned) perception → task planning → motion planning → trajectory → joint servo stack, and the specific conditions under which a LEARNED low-level "cerebellum" beats classical IK + impedance control

-- KEY TAKEAWAYS --
 * BLUNT ANSWER TO THE KEY QUESTION: as literally specified (desired EE pose + current joints → joint commands), Model B is inverse kinematics plus a servo loop. This is MoveIt Servo. It is solved exactly, runs at 100–1000 Hz with microsecond-to-millisecond latency, achieves sub-millimeter accuracy, generates itself from the URDF with zero training data, and is verifiable. A learned replacement will be slower (10–50 Hz on a Mac mini), less accurate (millimeters to centimeters), unverifiable, and must be retrained per robot. On the proposal's own stated axis of 'improve control rate', the learned Model B is strictly worse.
 * The proposal's stated goal of splitting capacity to raise control rate is based on a false premise. Control rate in the classical stack is high *because the innermost layer is not learned*. Two neural networks in series have a total latency that is the SUM of two forward passes plus an IPC hop; that is slower than one network, not faster. The only way a two-level split raises effective rate is the Helix pattern: a big slow model at 7–9 Hz feeding a TINY fast model at 200 Hz. That requires Model B to be ~1000× smaller than Model A, not a co-equal second model.
 * The proposal chose the right SPLIT POINT (Cartesian) but the wrong INTERFACE. A bare SE(3) pose is an information bottleneck that discards exactly what a learned low level would need: which null-space/elbow configuration is wanted, how stiff to be, what contact is expected, and what task phase we are in. Helix passes a continuous latent and backprops S1→S2 through it precisely to avoid this. If you keep a pose-only interface, classical IK is provably the best possible Model B and learning cannot help.
 * Learned low-level genuinely beats classical in a short, specific list, and NONE of the items on that list are 'converting pose to joints': (1) redundancy/posture preference — best evidence is ERJ 80–100% vs pure task-space 0–40% real-world success in confined spaces; (2) variable impedance for contact — VICES and follow-ups, but ONLY if the hardware exposes torque or stiffness; (3) unmodeled dynamics (friction, backlash, cable stretch) — Nguyen-Tuong/Peters LWPR/LGP, but these are tiny proprioception-only regressors at 1 kHz; (4) closing the loop on images to absorb calibration and perception error — i.e. visual servoing; (5) deformables and (6) floating-base whole-body balance, neither of which applies to a fixed-base desk arm.
 * Residual learning is the dominant hybrid pattern and is the correct redesign: π = π_classical + π_learned, with the learned term additive in the SAME space as the classical command and clipped for safety. Johannink et al. ICRA 2019 (block insertion), Silver et al. 2018, Davchev et al. RA-L 2022 (peg insertion): ~80–100% success on contact-rich insertion with thousands of real samples and 3–5× data efficiency over RL from scratch, plus graceful degradation (residual → 0 recovers the classical controller).
 * Classical IK is not one thing and the proposal should not be evaluated against the weak version. IKFast gives closed-form solutions in ~5–50 µs. TRAC-IK solves >99.8% of queries vs ~96% for stock KDL (KDL's Newton method fails at joint limits) in sub-millisecond time. QP-based IK (iKinQP, TSID, pink) additionally enforces collision avoidance, joint limits, and prioritized multi-task objectives as hard constraints — something a neural network can only approximate.
 * The hardware command interface is a hard gate on the compliance argument. If the arm accepts only joint POSITION commands (typical for hobbyist and most industrial arms), a learned Model B outputting joint positions has exactly the same expressive power as IK + position servo and can add nothing on contact tasks. The contact-compliance case for learning requires a torque or variable-stiffness interface (Franka FCI, KUKA FRI, quasi-direct-drive actuators).
 * Model A is the well-motivated half. Replacing hand-engineered perception + symbol grounding + grasp-pose databases with a learned RGB → target-pose model is exactly where learning has unambiguously beaten the classical stack. Concentrating capacity there, and using classical MoveIt Servo / TRAC-IK / impedance control underneath, is a defensible and cheap architecture.

-- IMPLICATIONS --
 > Baseline you must beat before writing any Model B: Model A (learned, RGB → target EE pose at 5–10 Hz) → MoveIt Servo or TRAC-IK with nullspace posture bias → ros2_control at 500–1000 Hz → Cartesian impedance if the hardware supports it. This is a weekend of integration, needs zero training data for the low level, and is the configuration that OpenVLA/π0/RT-2 deployments actually use. If the split-architecture experiment does not beat this, it has demonstrated nothing.
 > Minimal correct fix for the interface bottleneck, in increasing order of cost: (1) have Model A emit pose + one redundancy scalar (arm angle φ or the base joint), which the ERJ result suggests is worth 0–40% → 80–100% success in confined spaces, at zero latency cost; (2) add a 6-D stiffness vector (VICES pattern); (3) add a small learned residual on top of the classical output. Only after all three fail should a full learned Model B be considered.
 > If you keep Model B, do not train it to output absolute joint commands from a pose. Train it as a RESIDUAL: Δq (or Δτ) added to the classical IK/impedance output, clipped to a hard safety envelope. This gives you a small network (fast on Apple silicon), a working system on day zero, an interpretable ablation (residual gain 0 → 1), and no catastrophic failure mode.
 > The RGB input to Model B is either the whole justification or a serious liability, and you should decide which. If Model B uses images to correct pose error from calibration/perception drift, it is a learned visual servo — legitimate, but then benchmark it against classical uncalibrated IBVS with online Jacobian estimation, run it at camera rate, and say so explicitly. If Model B does not actually need images, remove them: they are the dominant compute cost and the dominant reason it cannot run fast.
 > Rate budget on an Apple-silicon Mac mini, realistically: a VLM-class Model A will run at roughly 1–5 Hz; a small ViT+transformer Model B at maybe 10–40 Hz; neither can enter the ros2_control loop. Plan for Model A ≈ 1–5 Hz, Model B ≈ 20–50 Hz, classical interpolation + servo at 500–1000 Hz. That is a THREE-level pyramid, and the bottom level must remain non-learned. Action chunking (ACT/π0 style) is the standard mitigation for the top two levels' latency and should be adopted regardless.
 > Reframe the split's justification. 'Split capacity across two models to improve performance' is not supported by the literature — Helix, GR00T N1, and Fast-in-Slow are jointly trained through a latent, and their benefit comes from the RATE asymmetry and from reusing internet pretraining in the slow half, not from parameter partitioning. Two independently trained models with a frozen pose interface will most likely underperform one end-to-end model of the same total size, because errors at the interface are uncorrectable downstream. If you want the split, plan to backprop through the interface or make the interface differentiable/latent.
 > Be explicit that the 'cerebellum' biological analogy argues against the current design, not for it. The biological cerebellum is a forward/inverse DYNAMICS model and error-correction machine operating on proprioception at high bandwidth — that maps onto learned inverse-dynamics compensation (LWPR/LGP at 1 kHz, tiny, no vision), not onto an RGB-conditioned pose-to-joint network at 20 Hz.
 > There is a real, cheap win available on cheap hardware: if the arm is a serial-bus servo or cable-driven arm with backlash, stiction, and gravity sag, a small learned dynamics/deflection compensator (a few thousand parameters, proprioception only, running at the servo rate) will improve tracking measurably. This is the one place where 'learned cerebellum' is both true to the biology and supported by evidence — and it is a completely different model than the one proposed.

### EDGE :: Local/edge deployment feasibility of running two neural policies concurrently for real-time robot arm control — Apple Silicon Mac mini vs Jetson Orin/Thor vs desktop RTX — evaluated against a proposed "cerebrum (RGB+EE pose → target EE pose) / cerebellum (target pose + joints + RGB → joint commands)" dual-brain split

-- KEY TAKEAWAYS --
 * The dual-rate idea is correct and industry-standard (Helix 7-9 Hz / 200 Hz; GR00T ~10 Hz / 50-200 Hz; RoboDual 3.8x control-rate gain with a 20M specialist; HiRT 4.1 -> 9.8 Hz and 48% -> 75% on dynamic tasks). Your instinct is well supported. The specific INTERFACE you chose is where the literature disagrees with you.
 * Every successful published dual-system passes a LEARNED LATENT (Helix: continuous latent; HiRT: attention-pooled final-layer embedding; GR00T: cross-attention KV; RoboDual: latent PLUS discretized action). None passes only an SE(3) target pose. A 7-dim pose is a severe bottleneck that discards approach direction, grasp affordance, expected contact, and recovery intent.
 * On a fixed-base arm, 'target EE pose + joint values -> joint commands' is inverse kinematics, which is solved exactly in microseconds. A neural Model B is only justified if it does something IK cannot: null-space/collision avoidance, compliance, contact reaction, or backlash compensation. As specified, Model B may be learning an expensive approximation of a solved problem.
 * Your Model B takes RGB, which means you pay the compute-bound vision-encoder cost TWICE. That is precisely the axis on which Apple Silicon is weakest (M4 Pro ~7 FP16 TFLOPS vs RTX 4090's 165). Share one vision encoder, or give Model B proprioception + wrench + at most a small wrist crop.
 * MLX cannot run two models concurrently on the GPU. CITED: single Metal dispatch queue per process; issue #3078 (concurrent models from separate threads) crashes and was closed unresolved in Jan 2026; multi-process MLX hangs ~1 in 200-400 calls under GPU saturation. Apple has no equivalent of CUDA priority streams or MPS. Two MLX processes = head-of-line blocking, so your fast loop's worst-case latency equals your slow model's inference time.
 * The only architecture that makes concurrency physically real on a Mac: cerebrum in MLX on the GPU, cerebellum compiled to Core ML pinned to the ANE (cpuAndNeuralEngine, GPU explicitly excluded). CITED precedent: 'CoreML + MLX ... ANE and GPU run in parallel with no serialization.' You must verify ANE residency empirically, since Core ML silently falls back to GPU on unsupported ops.
 * Realistic Mac mini numbers (ESTIMATED by scaling cited RTX 4090 measurements by ~8-15x for prefill, ~3.5-4x for decode): a pi0-class 2.7B VLA ~2-5 Hz on M4 Pro, ~1.5-2 Hz on M4 base, ~1-1.5 Hz on M2 base. OpenVLA-7B ~1.5-2 Hz on M4 Pro. A 1-3B VLM TTFT with one image ~200-400 ms on M4 Pro. A 100-300M ViT+few-step-flow expert ~15-40 ms (25-60 Hz). A <50M proprio MLP ~1-3 ms on CPU/ANE (300-500 Hz, but with macOS scheduling jitter of +/-1-5 ms).
 * Reference points on real edge hardware (CITED): Jetson Thor runs pi0 at 52.6 ms / 19 Hz, pi0.5 at 44 ms / 23 Hz, GR00T N1.6 at 41-45 ms / 22-24 Hz. Jetson AGX Orin runs GR00T N1 at under 5 Hz. RTX 4090 runs pi0 at 31 ms / 32 Hz; RTX 5090 at 17.6 ms / 57 Hz. A Mac mini is roughly 1/8th of Thor on this workload.
 * Action chunking is nearly free and you are probably under-using it. CITED: growing the chunk from 5 to 250 actions costs only ~11% more end-to-end latency, while going from 10 to 50 denoising steps costs 5x. So: long chunks, few flow-matching steps, never an autoregressive token action head (102x penalty vs diffusion). LeRobot already ships async serving + weighted-average temporal ensembling and already supports --policy_device=mps.
 * Manipulation needs three tiers, not two: semantics 0.5-10 Hz, policy setpoints 20-50 Hz (5-10 Hz causes force oscillation in contact), servo/impedance 500-2000 Hz on the arm controller. Also, every cited contact-rich system closes its fast loop on FORCE/WRENCH/TACTILE data, not on RGB. And a camera-fed Model B is capped at camera frame rate (30-60 Hz) no matter how fast the GPU is.
 * macOS as a control host, bluntly: ROS 2 macOS is Tier 3 (CITED REP-2000) - source build only, no binaries, no CI testing. No SocketCAN (XNU/BSD); userspace CAN drivers give ~800-1500 Hz send-reply. No EtherCAT master, no PREEMPT_RT, no isolcpus, no CUDA/TensorRT, no discrete GPU option on a mini. It is a good ML inference and development box and a bad real-time control box.
 * Cost-benefit reality check: a used RTX 3090/4090 desktop on the same wired switch runs your cerebrum ~8-10x faster than an M4 Pro Mac mini, supports genuine concurrent multi-model execution via priority streams, and adds essentially zero latency (CITED: 31.1 ms over 1G Ethernet vs 31.06 ms on-device). WiFi 6 costs only ~4 ms.

-- IMPLICATIONS --
 > Change the interface from a bare SE(3) target pose to a learned latent, or at minimum to (pose + latent), following RoboDual which passes both. If you must keep an interpretable interface for debugging, pass a short pose TRAJECTORY (a chunk of 8-16 waypoints with timing) plus a latent side-channel, not a single waypoint. A single 7-dim waypoint at 3 Hz makes your whole system 3 Hz-reactive.
 > Reconsider whether you want two MODELS or one model with two RATES. GR00T N1's shape - one VLM backbone whose embeddings are cached, plus a cheap action head that re-runs many times against the cached embeddings via cross-attention - gives you the entire latency benefit inside ONE MLX process, with no Metal queue contention, no doubled vision cost, and no IPC. On Apple Silicon specifically this is strictly better engineering than two processes.
 > Do not give Model B a full RGB stream. It doubles your compute-bound vision cost on the platform least able to afford it, and it caps Model B at camera frame rate anyway. Feed Model B: the interface from A, joint positions/velocities, and (if you have it) an F/T or wrench signal. Add a small wrist-camera crop only if you can show it earns its latency.
 > Exhaust action chunking + RTC + temporal ensembling BEFORE building the second model. A single 2-3B VLA at 3 Hz emitting 50-action chunks with real-time chunking already gives you a smooth 30-50 Hz command stream. That satisfies the control-rate half of your goal with zero architectural risk. Then measure whether the split adds anything on success rate and generalization - which is a separate, testable claim.
 > Your stated rationale - 'instead of pouring all parameters into one model, split the capacity across two' - is NOT what the supporting literature tested. RoboDual is 7B + 20M (350:1) and the small side is the only part trained. Helix is 7B + 80M. HiRT is 7B + 35-150M. Every win came from ADDING a small fast head to a large frozen generalist, not from halving a fixed budget. Design for a heavily asymmetric split, not a balanced one.
 > Train Model B on STALE Model A outputs from day one. HiRT explicitly caches the latest latent and lets the low level consume it while the VLM runs asynchronously. If you train B on synchronous ground-truth poses and deploy it against a 300 ms-old pose from a Mac-hosted Model A, expect overshoot and limit-cycle oscillation near contact - and you will misdiagnose it as a modelling problem.
 > If you stay on the Mac, the mandatory implementation constraints are: (1) cerebrum in MLX on GPU, cerebellum in Core ML pinned to ANE (or CPU/BNNS) - never two MLX-on-GPU processes; (2) verify ANE residency with the Core ML performance report and powermetrics, do not assume it; (3) quantize the cerebrum to int4 for FOOTPRINT not speed; (4) budget 16 GB as the floor and only if the cerebrum is <=3B at int4 - a 7B bf16 VLA (15 GB) will not co-reside with anything.
 > Do not run the servo loop on the Mac. Push the >=500 Hz joint/impedance loop onto the arm's own controller or a small Linux/microcontroller host, and let the Mac emit 20-50 Hz setpoints over Ethernet. This is what every cited system does (1-2 kHz impedance + 10-60 Hz policy) and it makes macOS's lack of real-time scheduling a non-issue.
 > Instrument jitter before you trust any of this. Log per-cycle end-to-end latency histograms (p50/p95/p99/max), not means. The cited Orin result (150.5 ms mean, 0.13 ms std, 150.4-151.0 ms range) is what a good stack looks like. If your Mac setup shows p99 several times p50, you have found the Metal serialization problem and you must move the fast model off the GPU.
 > Test the dual-brain hypothesis first on quasi-static tasks vs dynamic ones separately. HiRT's hierarchy showed comparable results on static benchmarks and a 48% -> 75% jump only on dynamic ones. If your target tasks are quasi-static tabletop pick-and-place, the split may buy you nothing on success rate - and you should know that before investing in the architecture.
 > Pragmatic recommendation: develop on the Mac mini inside LeRobot's async server/client harness (it already supports --policy_device=mps, action chunks, and weighted-average ensembling over a USB serial arm), then move the policy server to a Linux box with an RTX GPU or a Jetson Thor for anything requiring >10 Hz or contact-rich behaviour. Keeping the Mac as the policy SERVER and a Linux host as the robot CLIENT over Ethernet costs ~0-4 ms and removes every macOS real-time objection.
